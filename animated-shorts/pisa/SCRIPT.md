# "Why the Leaning Tower of Pisa Never Falls Over" — script v1 (2026-11-10)

Built to REFERENCE_STUDY.md: 14-word paradox hook, one protagonist (the tower itself, with a face), escalation
beats that are each one picture, the "You see," turn at ~55-60 %, an everyday analogy, one callback ending.

Facts (with sources):
- Construction began August 1173; by the third storey (~1178) the tower was already leaning. Soft subsoil
  (clay/sand) and a shallow ~3 m foundation. (Wikipedia "Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Discover Magazine; History Hit)
- Wars halted construction for almost a century, which let the soil consolidate; work resumed in 1272 and the
  upper floors were built taller on one side to compensate, so the tower is curved. (Wikipedia; Discover)
- Closed to the public on 7 January 1990 because the tilt (about 5.5 degrees) had become dangerous.
  (History.com; Wikipedia)
- 1993-2001: lead counterweights on the north (high) side, then soil extracted from beneath the north side; the
  tilt was reduced by about 45 cm to 3.97 degrees; reopened December 2001; engineers expect it to be stable for
  at least 200 years. (Ingenia "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Scientific American; Wikipedia)

## Voice-over
The Leaning Tower of Pisa started falling over in 1178, and it still hasn't landed.
It begins as a brand-new bell tower, built from shiny white marble, on a three-metre foundation.
Big mistake. Pisa sits on soft, wet clay, and by the third floor, one side is sinking.
Then war breaks out, and the builders put down their tools for almost a hundred years.
When they finally come back, they try to fix it by making the new floors taller on the short side.
So now the tower isn't just leaning. It's bent, like a banana.
By 1990, it's tipping five and a half degrees, and the tower is closed to the public.
So why hasn't it fallen? You see, a tower only tips over when its centre of weight slides past the edge of its base.
Ever leaned back on a chair? Fine, fine, fine, until one centimetre too far.
Pisa was getting close to that centimetre.
So engineers stacked hundreds of tonnes of lead on the high side, then carefully drilled the soil out from underneath it, a few buckets at a time.
Slowly, the tower sank back forty-five centimetres.
Engineers say it's safe for at least two hundred more years. So it still hasn't landed. Give it time.
