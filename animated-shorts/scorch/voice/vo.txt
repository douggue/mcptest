A skyscraper in London melted a man's car without ever touching it.
It's the summer of 2013, and the newest tower in the city is almost finished: glass, shiny, and curved like a giant spoon.
A businessman parks his brand new Jaguar on the street right below it.
Two hours later, he comes back.
The wing mirror has melted, the badge is drooping, and the panels have gone soft like toffee.
Down the road, a doormat starts smoking, and a bike seat blisters.
So a reporter does the only sensible thing. He walks into the beam with a frying pan, and cooks an egg.
Wait, how does a building make breakfast?
You see, a flat window bounces sunlight straight back, nice and spread out.
But this tower's face curves inwards.
Ever burned a leaf with a magnifying glass? A curved mirror does the same trick. It catches sunlight over a huge area, and squeezes it into one tiny, burning spot.
And this mirror was a hundred and sixty metres tall.
The owners paid for the car, and bolted a giant sunshade across the glass.
Luckily, it was the architect's first time.
Unluckily, it wasn't. Three years earlier, his hotel in Las Vegas was frying tourists by the pool.
Two years later, the tower won an award: Britain's worst new building.
