# "The Building That Cooked Breakfast" v2 (2026-11-02)

Rebuild of v1: tighter script, Kokoro voice (af_heart, speed 1.3), 18 shots with acting.

Facts (with sources):
- 20 Fenchurch Street, City of London ("Walkie-Talkie"), ~160 m, by architect Rafael Viñoly; concave south facade.
- Late Aug 2013 (29 Aug): Jaguar XJ owner parked on Eastcheap; ~2 h later the wing mirror, badge and panels had
  melted. Developers (Land Securities / Canary Wharf) paid the £946 repair. (ITV, Construction Enquirer, Time, 2013-09-03)
- Hot spot also blistered a bicycle seat and burned a hole in a doormat; a City A.M. reporter fried an egg in it.
  Press nicknames "Walkie-Scorchie" / "Fryscraper". (National Geographic, NBC News, CS Monitor, Sept 2013)
- A temporary screen went up within days, later a permanent brise-soleil (horizontal fins) on the south face.
- Viñoly's Vdara hotel, Las Vegas (opened Dec 2009): 2010 "death ray" reflected off its concave facade scorched
  hair and melted plastic cups at the pool. (Wikipedia "Vdara"; Gizmodo "A brief history of buildings that melt things")
- 2015: the tower won the Carbuncle Cup for the UK's worst new building. (Fox News / Building Design)

## Voice-over (~223 words, 63.8 s)
A skyscraper in London melted a man's car, without ever touching it.
It's 2013, and the City's newest tower is nearly finished. Thirty-seven floors of shiny glass, curved like a giant spoon.
A businessman parks his brand-new Jaguar on the street right below it.
Two hours later, he comes back.
The wing mirror has melted. The badge is drooping. And the panels have gone soft, like toffee.
Down the road, a shop's doormat starts smoking, and a bike seat blisters.
So a reporter does the only sensible thing. He walks into the light with a frying pan, and cooks an egg.
Wait. How does a building make breakfast?
You see, a flat window bounces sunlight straight back, nice and spread out.
But this tower's face curves inwards, like a satellite dish.
Ever burned a leaf with a magnifying glass? Same trick. The curve catches sunlight over a huge area, and squeezes it into one tiny, burning spot.
And this magnifying glass was a hundred and sixty metres tall.
The owners paid for the car, and bolted a giant sunshade across the glass.
Luckily, the architect would never make that mistake twice.
Unluckily, this already was twice. Three years earlier, his hotel in Las Vegas was frying tourists by the pool.
And two years later, the tower won an award: Britain's worst new building.
